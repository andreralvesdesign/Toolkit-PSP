Aggressive behaviour

Aggressive behaviour can arise as a maladaptive attempt to express frustration, defend needs or regain a sense of control. Although the person’s emotion may be valid, aggressive behaviour can undermine safety, respect and the functioning of relationships.

This module deals with difficult situations. If you need to, take a break or use the Support button, always available at the top of the page.

7 screens

First of all

Understanding the causes of aggressive behaviour does not mean accepting or normalising it.

Safety, respect and interpersonal boundaries must always be protected in the workplace.

What may be happening?

Difficulty regulating emotions or coping with frustration

Aggression may be associated with difficulty regulating emotions, coping with frustration or interpreting situations as threatening.

The person may

  • feel wronged, undervalued or out of control
  • interpret comments or situations as personal attacks
  • react intensely to stress or frustration
  • have difficulty communicating needs effectively
  • use aggression as an attempt to be heard
  • be emotionally overloaded by personal or professional difficulties

Factors that may increase the likelihood

  • accumulated stress
  • fatigue
  • interpersonal conflict
  • very demanding environments
  • noise, heat or sensory overload
  • alcohol or substance use

How to act

What to do?

The priority is to reduce emotional escalation, ensure safety and promote effective communication.

  1. 1
    Keep self-control: Speak calmly, firmly and respectfully, avoiding increasing emotional tension.
  2. 2
    Assess safety: Make sure there is no immediate risk to yourself or to other people.
  3. 3
    Set clear limits: Communicate objectively that aggressive behaviour is not acceptable. “I want to help resolve this, but I can’t continue this conversation while you are speaking like that.”
  4. 4
    Validate the emotion, not the behaviour: Acknowledge frustration or distress without legitimising aggression. “I understand that you are very angry.”
  5. 5
    Reduce escalation: If necessary, suggest a break, change of space, or picking up the conversation later.
  6. 6
    Use simple, objective communication: Focus on facts, avoid accusations, talk about concrete needs.
  7. 7
    Repeat limits calmly: If the person provokes or insists, repeat your position without getting into confrontation (“broken record”).
  8. 8
    Use a non-threatening posture: Hands visible, neutral tone, open posture, calm facial expression.
  9. 9
    Focus on resolution: Once emotionally stable, steer the conversation towards practical solutions.

Points to watch

What to avoid?

  1. Retaliating or responding with aggression: Sarcasm, irony or direct confrontation tend to increase conflict.
  2. Humiliating or exposing the person: Avoid public confrontations or belittling comments.
  3. Getting into power struggles: Trying to “win” the argument can worsen the loss of control.
  4. Judging or labelling: Avoid phrases such as “you’re aggressive” or “you’re out of control”.
  5. Assuming intentions: Focus on observable behaviours instead of presuming motives.
  6. Ignoring persistent aggressive behaviour: The absence of limits can reinforce the behaviour.
  7. Giving in to inappropriate demands: If aggression consistently results in a benefit, it tends to be repeated.

Specialist support

When to refer?

Referral to HR, Occupational Medicine or psychological support should be considered when:

  • !There is a risk to physical integrity
  • !There are threats, intimidation or recurring aggression
  • !The aggressive behaviour is persistent and hard to control
  • !There is a significant impact on the work environment or the team
  • !There are signs of serious psychological distress, substance abuse or suicidal ideation
  • !Mediation and conflict management strategies are no longer effective
