Principles of the approach

An effective approach rests on

  • Creating psychological safety
  • Empathetic communication
  • Active listening
  • Clarity about the limits of the leader’s role

The aim is not to solve the problem, but to make it easier for the person to open up and to be referred appropriately.

    Supportive phrases and what to avoid

    Phrases to sustain and validate the person throughout the dialogue (different from the opening ones).

    Supportive phrases during the conversation
    • “I’m available to support you.”
    • “Your wellbeing matters.”
    • “You don’t have to deal with this on your own.”
    • “What you’re feeling makes sense, given what is happening.”
    What to avoid
    • Minimising the situation (“it could be worse”, “calm down”).
    • Pressing for answers, details or explanations.
    • Offering immediate solutions without listening first.
    • Interpreting or diagnosing (“I think you’re burned out”).
    • Taking on a clinical role.
    • Guaranteeing absolute confidentiality in situations of risk (when it may be necessary to involve other resources).

    Case studies with leader intervention

    Four situations, four interventions

    Click on a case, think about how you would approach it and click on “How can the leader step in?” to see the intervention.

    Case study 1An employee with a consistent track record starts missing deadlines and is frequently distracted.

    1. Initial approach: the leader starts a fact-based conversation, using phrases such as: “I’ve noticed some recent difficulty in meeting deadlines and I wanted to understand how you are.”
    2. Exploration: lets the employee explain the context (e.g. overload, personal matters, difficulty concentrating).
    3. Practical adjustments, depending on the situation: reprioritise tasks; temporarily reduce the workload; break complex tasks into stages; clarify objectives.
    4. Defining a plan: set realistic short-term goals; agree follow-up points.
    5. Follow-up: reassess progress after a few days/weeks.
    Case study 2An employee reacts disproportionately to feedback and is irritable.

    1. Structured approach: “I noticed that in our last few interactions you’ve been reacting quite intensely. I’d like to understand what’s going on.”
    2. Validation: acknowledge that there may be underlying factors (stress, frustration, overload).
    3. Clarifying limits: separate emotion from behaviour, for example: “I understand you’re under pressure, but we need to ensure a respectful environment in the team.”
    4. Exploring needs: identify sources of stress; adjust workload or expectations, if necessary.
    5. Strategies: suggest breaks; encourage moments of self-regulation before responding.
    6. Monitoring: follow progress and reinforce adjusted behaviour.
    Case study 3An employee works excessively, avoids breaks and shows clear signs of exhaustion.

    1. Identification and validation: “I’ve noticed you’ve taken on a very heavy workload and you seem tired to me.”
    2. Normalisation: confirm that overload has an impact and is not sustainable.
    3. Organisational intervention: redistribute tasks; set limits on working hours; encourage real breaks; review priorities.
    4. Reinforcing culture: convey that productivity should not come at the expense of wellbeing.
    5. Referral: suggest specialist support, if necessary.
    6. Ongoing follow-up: monitor signs of recovery or deterioration.
    Case study 4An employee significantly reduces interaction with the team and seems distant.

    1. Careful approach: “I’ve sensed you’ve been more distant from the team lately and I wanted to understand how you are.”
    2. Creating a safe space: let the person speak at their own pace, without pressure.
    3. Exploration: identify whether it is demotivation, a personal difficulty or a conflict in the team.
    4. Intervention: facilitate gradual reintegration; promote safe interactions (e.g. smaller meetings); adjust social expectations.
    5. Ongoing support: reinforce availability and keep in regular contact.

    Psychological First Aid mini-section for leaders

    When an employee is in distress: what to do in a first approach

    The aim of Psychological First Aid is to reduce initial arousal, offer a safe presence, listen with empathy, support immediate needs and make it easier to access additional help when necessary. The leader does not need to have all the answers, nor to “solve” the situation in the moment.

    Psychological First Aid rests on principles consistent with a trauma-informed approach: safety, trust, choice, collaboration, empowerment and respect for human dignity. In practice, this means protecting the person from additional stress, taking care of immediate needs, offering emotional and practical support, normalising common reactions to difficult situations and connecting the person to sources of support when that makes sense.

    For a leader, the priority is often to follow a simple logic: observe, listen and connect.

    • 1Observe
    • 2Listen
    • 3Connect

    It is also important to distinguish support from intrusion. Stepping in early does not mean pressing. It means being available and offering help without forcing a conversation, without insisting on details and without taking away the person’s autonomy.

    Psychological First Aid mini-section for leaders

    To have at hand in the conversation

    • “I’ve noticed some changes and I wanted to understand how you are.”
    • “You don’t have to explain everything now.”
    • “I’m here to listen, if you’d like to talk.”
    • “What you’re feeling makes complete sense, given what has happened.”
    • “Right now, let’s focus on what can help today.”
    • “Would you like me to help you work out what you can do next?”
    • “If it makes sense, I can help you access support.”
    • Privacy and containment: approach the person in a quiet place, away from unnecessary exposure.
    • Active listening: rephrase, clarify, validate and tolerate silence.
    • Immediate practical support: find out whether the person needs a break, to leave the area, to adjust tasks, support from someone they trust or information about available resources.
    • Appropriate referral: recognise when the situation goes beyond the leader’s role and make it easier to connect to additional support.
    • Follow-up: get back in touch with the person to show continuity and not abandonment.

    Phrases

    • “Calm down.”
    • “It could be worse.”
    • “Don’t feel like that.”
    • “I know exactly how you feel.”
    • “You’ve been through worse.”

    Attitudes

    • Pressing for details.
    • Trying to interpret or diagnose.
    • Giving immediate solutions without listening.
    • Judging, minimising or moralising.
    • Making false promises.
    • Insisting on helping when the person does not want it.
    • Guaranteeing absolute confidentiality when there is a risk and it may be necessary to involve other resources.
