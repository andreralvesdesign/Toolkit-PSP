What the evidence suggests

Psychological safety is built through everyday experience with leadership

It does not depend only on formal policies or procedures. Qualitative studies consistently point to clear differences between psychologically safe and unsafe contexts.

Contexts with greater psychological safety
  • Open and accessible communication
  • Relationships of trust with leadership
  • Room to ask questions and ask for help
  • Leaders who listen and value contributions
  • Openness to discussing mistakes and difficulties
  • Active participation in continuous improvement

People feel more comfortable speaking up, contributing and taking interpersonal risks without fear of humiliation or punishment.

Contexts with lower psychological safety
  • Fear of negative consequences when speaking up
  • Excessive criticism or defensive responses
  • Marked hierarchical distance
  • Lack of response to, or dismissal of, concerns
  • Avoidance of feedback, questions or flagging mistakes
  • A culture of silence or withdrawal

People are more likely to hide difficulties, avoid asking for support or stop actively contributing.

Practical implications for leaders

Functional and relational skills

Building psychological safety depends on combining both.

Functional skills

They relate to how work is organised and run:

  • Clarity of objectives and expectations
  • Structure and coordination
  • Decision-making
  • Managing priorities
  • Consistency in how you act
Relational skills

They relate to how people experience leadership:

  • Active listening
  • Availability and accessibility
  • Respectful communication
  • Validating contributions
  • Ability to respond without judgement or punishment
  • Constructive handling of mistakes and conflict

The evidence suggests that more psychologically safe teams tend to emerge when both dimensions coexist.

Leader self-check

Ten questions to ask yourself

  1. 1Is psychological health an explicit part of how I lead, or does it only come up when there is a problem?
  2. 2Do I have indicators to understand absenteeism, presenteeism, conflict, overtime and wear and tear in the team?
  3. 3Does my team know what is a priority and what can be renegotiated?
  4. 4Do people feel they can disagree, ask for help or admit a mistake without fear?
  5. 5Am I responding to concerns with openness, or with defensiveness and haste?
  6. 6Do I hold regular 1:1s that focus not only on tasks, but also on the experience of work?
  7. 7Am I rewarding constant availability and over-exertion, even without meaning to?
  8. 8Can I tell a performance problem from a possible sign of psychological distress?
  9. 9Does the team know the support resources and escalation routes?
  10. 10What in my own way of leading may be unintentionally increasing psychosocial risk?

Leader self-care as a preventive measure

Leader self-care is not an “individualistic” topic; it is an operational variable. Exhausted leaders tend to communicate worse, listen less, react with irritation, increase control and reduce emotional availability.

So leaders should model healthy behaviours, take breaks, respect limits, avoid glorifying overwork and normalise seeking help.

Self-care questions for the leader

  • Have I been sleeping and recovering enough to make decisions clearly?
  • Am I permanently in urgency mode?
  • Have I been responding with irritation, impatience or withdrawal?
  • Am I demanding a pace from the team that I already know is not sustainable for me?
  • Do I have regular space for supervision, reflection or peer support?
  • Am I using breaks, limits and delegation as real practices, or only as talk?
  • What do I need to adjust this week to lead with more presence and less reactivity?

Self-care strategies

These are simple, intentional actions that help a person look after their physical and emotional wellbeing day to day and reduce overload in the short term. Although they are important, they do not replace specialist support when there are signs of risk. They include actions such as:

  • Encouraging basic routines: sleep, eating, movement
  • Promoting breaks and moments of rest
  • Staying connected with people you trust
  • Using simple regulation strategies: breathing, grounding, relaxing activities
  • Respecting your own pace (without pressure to “feel better” quickly)

See the self-regulation strategies (3.2)

Limits of the leader’s role

Presence without intrusion, support without control, listening without judgement.

The leader can be a key figure for initial stabilisation, but does not replace clinical or specialist support. Their role is to recognise signs, approach in a human way, reduce additional harm, support practical decisions and refer when necessary. Leading for psychological safety therefore means combining structure and humanity.

  • Clarify priorities
  • Manage workloads
  • Create safe spaces to speak
  • Listen actively
  • Respond with respect
  • Ensure concrete support

Psychological safety day to day

Psychological safety is built in daily micro-interactions

In demanding organisational contexts, preventing psychosocial risk does not depend only on formal policies or programmes. It depends, to a large extent, on people’s everyday relational experience at work: how they are heard, treated and supported when something is not right.

The evidence suggests that psychological safety is built in daily micro-interactions, through leaders who are accessible, consistent, responsive and non-punitive, but also through clear, fair and well-organised working conditions.

When structure and humanity come together, people tend to speak up earlier, ask for help with less fear, cooperate better and stay more connected to the team and to the work.
