Part 5 · Sensitive Situations

Serious personal crisis

A personal crisis can arise when a significant life situation temporarily goes beyond a person’s usual resources for coping, causing emotional distress and an impact on daily functioning.

This module deals with difficult situations. If you need to, take a break or use the Support button, always available at the top of the page.

6 screens

What may be happening?

Difficult or potentially traumatic personal situations

The employee may be dealing with situations such as:

  • the death of a significant person
  • divorce or family conflict
  • financial or legal problems
  • a diagnosis of serious illness
  • exposure to traumatic events
  • major life changes
  • overload associated with new responsibilities

Going through one of these experiences may be accompanied by a perception of ineffectiveness, a lack of resources or the failure of the usual coping and problem-solving mechanisms.

How to act

What to do?

The priority is to offer presence, flexibility and practical support without invading the person’s privacy.

  1. 1
    Show availability and empathy: Listen without judgement and respect the person’s pace. Some people may not want to talk at that moment, and that should be respected.
  2. 2
    Validate the experience: Acknowledge that the situation may be difficult or emotionally demanding.
  3. 3
    Temporarily relax expectations: Whenever possible, adjust tasks, relax deadlines, reduce additional pressure.
  4. 4
    Offer practical support: Help identify immediate needs or concrete difficulties.
  5. 5
    Encourage connection to the support network: Make it easier to contact family, friends, colleagues, available support resources.
  6. 6
    Inform about existing resources: Point out available support, such as psychological support, occupational medicine, flexible hours, social resources.

Points to watch

What to avoid?

  1. Pressing them to talk about personal details: Avoid intrusive questions or insisting on explanations.
  2. Minimising or invalidating distress: Avoid phrases such as “that will pass quickly” or “I’ve seen worse”.
  3. Demanding immediate normal functioning: Pressure to keep up the same level of productivity can increase distress and disorganisation.
  4. Pathologising human reactions: A personal crisis does not mean the presence of mental illness.
  5. Giving simplistic or unsolicited advice: The person is not always looking for immediate solutions; often they first need to be listened to and stabilised.

Specialist support

When to refer?

Referral to psychological or medical support should be considered when:

  • !There is significant distress, intense and persistent on most days, over a prolonged period of time
  • !There is a significant impact on personal and professional functioning
  • !There are indications of a mental health disorder such as depression, anxiety, prolonged grief or post-traumatic stress disorder
  • !There are risk behaviours or suicidal ideation/intent
  • !When the person asks for specialist help

Where to turn
