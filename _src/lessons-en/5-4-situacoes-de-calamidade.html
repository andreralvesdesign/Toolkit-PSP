Disaster situations

In the face of a sudden, serious or potentially traumatic event, intense stress reactions are to be expected. Psychological First Aid aims to reduce immediate distress, promote safety and support initial recovery.

This module deals with difficult situations. If you need to, take a break or use the Support button, always available at the top of the page.

It is important to remember

Normal people can have intense reactions to abnormal situations.

In the face of potentially traumatic events, the usual coping mechanisms may no longer be enough, causing a temporary psychological crisis.

What may be happening?

Common reactions

Reactions can vary between people. They are often normal responses to intense stress. Most people recover gradually when there is safety, support, stability and social connection.

Emotional

  • Emotional shock
  • Anxiety or panic
  • Fear
  • Anger
  • Guilt
  • Irritability
  • Despair
  • Emotional numbing
  • Vulnerability

Cognitive

  • Confusion
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Memory changes
  • Intrusive thoughts
  • Excessive worry
  • Difficulty making decisions
  • A feeling of disbelief or denial

Physical

  • Tachycardia
  • Hyperventilation
  • Hypertension
  • Trembling
  • Nausea
  • Fatigue
  • Insomnia
  • Hypervigilance
  • Changes in appetite

Behavioural

  • Agitation
  • Fight, flight or freeze responses
  • Isolation
  • Distrust
  • Giving up activities
  • Difficulties at work
  • Automatic or disorganised behaviours

How to act

What to do?

The intervention should be flexible, practical and focused on initial stabilisation.

  1. 1
    Ensure physical safety: Remove the person from danger and ensure basic needs, such as water, food, warm clothing, rest and contact with family.
  2. 2
    Reduce disturbing stimuli: Limit exposure to violent images, repeated news, and sounds or smells associated with the event.
  3. 3
    Establish a calm and empathetic presence: Introduce yourself calmly, ask permission to help, listen without pressing, avoid intrusive questions.
  4. 4
    Promote stabilisation and grounding: If the person is highly activated or disoriented, encourage slow breathing, help them focus on their surroundings, use simple grounding techniques. Example: “Can you name 5 things you can see right now?”
  5. 5
    Offer practical help: Support immediate needs, such as contacting family, organising information, solving urgent problems.
  6. 6
    Strengthen the connection to the support network: Make it easier to contact family, friends, colleagues, community resources.
  7. 7
    Normalise stress reactions: Explain that many intense reactions are expected after serious events.
  8. 8
    Promote a sense of control: Involve the person in small decisions and encourage active participation in their recovery.

Points to watch

What to avoid?

  1. Pathologising initial reactions: Avoid labelling acute stress responses as “illness” or “weakness”.
  2. Pressing them to talk about the trauma: Do not insist on details of the event or force emotional sharing.
  3. Using invalidating clichés: Avoid phrases such as “It could be worse”, “You have to be strong”, “At least you survived”.
  4. Giving false reassurance: Do not promise absolute safety without concrete information.
  5. Excessive exposure to the event: Avoid repeated contact with traumatic images, videos or accounts.
  6. With children: Do not criticise regressive behaviours. Avoid explanations that are too frightening or detailed.

Specialist support

When to refer?

Referral to specialist support should be considered when:

  • !There is a risk to themselves or to others
  • !There are signs of medical shock or an inability to respond
  • !The person remains seriously dysregulated despite stabilisation strategies
  • !There is a worsening of previous physical or psychological problems
  • !Difficulties persist for several weeks with a significant impact on daily functioning
  • !Alcohol or drug use increases
  • !There are intense symptoms of anxiety, dissociation or persistent distress
