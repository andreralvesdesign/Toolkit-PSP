Limits and confidentiality

Supporting someone means closeness, but also responsibility

Knowing how far to go, what to keep confidential and when to involve other resources is essential to protect both the person in distress and the one helping.

This balance avoids two common risks:

Risk 1

Overexposure
(breaking trust)

Risk 2

Taking on undue responsibility
(assuming a role that is not yours)

What is confidential?

In general, everything the person shares in a supportive context should be treated with respect and discretion.

This includes

  • personal or emotional content
  • difficult or traumatic experiences
  • information about family life, health or relationships

It is essential because it

  • creates safety for the person to talk
  • protects their dignity
  • strengthens trust in the relationship
Whenever possible, information should only be shared

with the person’s consent and with clarity about who it will be shared with and why.

When it may be necessary to share or act

Confidentiality is not absolute. There are situations in which not acting can be a greater risk.

When there are signs of

  • Risk to the person themselves (e.g. intense hopelessness, thoughts of self-harm, loss of control)
  • Risk to others (e.g. aggressive or dysregulated behaviour)
  • Serious situations in the workplace (e.g. harassment, violence, intense psychological distress)

In these cases, it is important to

  • not take on the responsibility alone
  • seek appropriate support (line manager, HR, health services, etc.)
  • wherever possible, tell the person what is going to be done
Example phrase

“I’m worried about you and I think it’s important that we involve more support. Can I help with that?”

What is the colleague’s role?

The role of the person offering support is not to replace a professional, but to offer initial support and make it easier to access help.

The colleague’s role includes

  • being present
  • listening and validating
  • helping to identify needs
  • making it easier to access resources

It also means

  • recognising personal limits
  • knowing when to stop
  • knowing when to refer
It is important to be clear that

Supporting is not clinical intervention. Supporting is not solving the problem. Supporting is not taking responsibility for the other person’s wellbeing.

    Limits and confidentiality

    Protecting yourself when you help

    Supporting someone in distress can have an emotional impact. So it is important to look after the person helping too.

    Some signs of overload
    • feeling responsible for the situation
    • difficulty “switching off” after the conversation
    • emotional strain or persistent worry
    In these cases, it may help to
    • share with someone you trust (without breaking confidentiality)
    • seek internal support (e.g. HR, supervision)
    • recognise that it is not possible to help alone

    Key message

    Helping does not mean taking on everything.

    Responsible support means respecting confidentiality, recognising limits and knowing when to involve other resources.

    Role play 1

    On the previous pages you saw what to do when a colleague is in distress. Now see how it happens in a real situation.

    ContextWarehouse. Days after witnessing a serious workplace accident, Maria shows clear signs of distress – withdrawal, difficulty concentrating, mistakes in simple tasks. A colleague approaches her.

    • What signs did you notice in Maria before the colleague approached her? Would you have identified them in your own work context?
    • The colleague did not try to solve the problem or give advice. How did you feel watching that restraint?
    • If you were Maria, what would have made a difference in that conversation?

    Role play 2

    Distress does not always come from work. Sometimes a colleague is coping with a loss or a serious personal concern – and yet they are still there, present, trying to carry on. The way to offer support is essentially the same, but the context calls for even greater care and less urgency to “sort things out”.

    ContextBreak room. An employee has just received news of a serious diagnosis for a close relative. A colleague notices that something is not right.

    • The colleague did not try to downplay it or offer solutions. What did she do instead?
    • When the employee said “it’s nothing much”, what was the reply? What would have happened if the other person had accepted that and left?
    • Is there anything in this conversation that seems hard to apply? Why?

    Practical conversation guide

    Six simple steps

    This guide organises the intervention in six steps, which help to structure the approach without making it rigid. You do not have to follow every step in a straight line.

    Quick guide: how to support a colleague

    Important notes

    • You do not need to “get everything right”. Consistency matters more than perfection.
    • Not every conversation moves forward; respecting the person’s pace is essential.
    • Small gestures (being present, listening) can have a big impact.

    Key message

    Helping a colleague does not require complex answers. It requires presence, structure and respect for the person’s pace.

    This guide is meant as support, not as a rigid rule.
