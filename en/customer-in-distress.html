Supporting a customer in distress

A customer in emotional distress may show greater sensitivity, reactivity or difficulty communicating needs in an organised way. The aim is not to provide psychotherapy, but to offer a human, safe and empathetic response within the professional role.

This module deals with difficult situations. If you need to, take a break or use the Support button, always available at the top of the page.

6 screens

What may be happening?

Emotionally demanding situations

The customer may be going through emotionally demanding personal or professional situations, and may show:

  • emotional hypersensitivity
  • anxiety, sadness or despair
  • irritability or anger
  • frustration with the service
  • intense emotional responses
  • a need to be heard, understood and validated

How to act

What to do?

  1. 1
    Show availability: Listen actively and respectfully, showing genuine interest in what the customer is communicating.
  2. 2
    Keep a calm, empathetic posture: Speak in a quiet, predictable and non-defensive way.
  3. 3
    Validate the distress: Acknowledge that the person’s experience may be difficult or emotionally intense. “I understand that this situation must be very difficult for you.”
  4. 4
    Clarify needs: Use open, non-intrusive questions to understand what happened, what the customer needs and how they can be helped at that moment.
  5. 5
    Help with problem-solving: Whenever possible, identify practical solutions, clarify next steps, reduce uncertainty.
  6. 6
    Manage expectations: Be transparent and realistic and avoid promising what you cannot guarantee.
  7. 7
    Use stabilisation strategies: If the customer is highly dysregulated: encourage slow breathing, use grounding, help refocus attention on the present.

Points to watch

What to avoid?

  1. Trying to “fix” the distress immediately: Pressure to make the customer feel better quickly can reduce the capacity for genuine listening.
  2. Invalidating or minimising: Avoid phrases such as “It’s not that bad”, “You need to calm down”.
  3. Assigning blame: Do not imply that the person’s distress is “their own fault”.
  4. Judging or labelling: Focus on the person’s need and distress, and not on what is “wrong” with them.
  5. Getting into a defensive confrontation: Avoid responding to the emotion with irritation, sarcasm or excessive rigidity.

Specialist support

When to make an urgent referral?

If there is:

  • !suicidal ideation or intent
  • !self-harming behaviour
  • !serious risk to the safety of the person or of others

In these cases

Ask for immediate support and contact the emergency services:

  • National Suicide Prevention Line
