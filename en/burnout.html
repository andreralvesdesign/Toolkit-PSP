Burnout

Burnout is a state of physical, emotional and mental exhaustion associated with chronic work stress.

This module deals with difficult situations. If you need to, take a break or use the Support button, always available at the top of the page.

7 screens

What may be happening?

Three main dimensions

Burnout often shows up through three main dimensions:

  • Individual levelEmotional exhaustionA feeling of physical and mental depletion, lack of energy, emotional wear and tear and difficulty coping with the demands of work and with other people.
  • Interpersonal levelDepersonalisation or cynicismEmotional distance from work, colleagues or customers, accompanied by irritability, coldness, cynicism or loss of involvement.
  • Self-assessment levelReduced personal accomplishmentA feeling of ineffectiveness, low professional self-esteem and a perception that work has lost meaning or purpose.

What may be happening?

Common warning signs

Physical

  • Exhaustion and lack of energy
  • Sleep changes
  • Headaches and muscle pain
  • Gastrointestinal problems
  • Changes in blood pressure

Psychological

  • Irritability and anxiety
  • Emotional distance
  • Negative feelings about work
  • Difficulty concentrating and with memory
  • A feeling of emptiness, hopelessness or ineffectiveness

Behavioural

  • Social isolation
  • Absenteeism
  • Reduced productivity
  • Greater tendency to make mistakes
  • Increased use of alcohol, tobacco or medication

What to do?

Strengthen protective resources

The intervention should focus on strengthening individual, organisational and relational protective resources.

Promote awareness

  • Recognise signs of exhaustion and accept the need to look after yourself

Develop coping strategies

  • Solve problems actively
  • Express needs and limits
  • Improve emotional communication
  • Ask for help when needed

Reinforce care of basic needs

  • Adequate sleep
  • Balanced diet
  • Regular physical exercise
  • Moments of leisure and rest
  • Following medical care

Set professional boundaries

  • Take regular breaks
  • Avoid frequent overtime
  • Do not work during holidays
  • Delegate tasks when possible
  • Promote balance between personal and professional life

Seek social support

  • Invest in meaningful relationships and keep a support network

Seek professional help

  • Assess the need for psychological or medical follow-up

Organisational strategies

  • Improve physical working conditions
  • Recognise performance
  • Promote autonomy
  • Invest in training
  • Make career progression easier
  • Encourage work-life balance

Strengthen interpersonal relationships: promote skills in

  • communication
  • leadership
  • conflict management
  • teamwork

Points to watch

What to avoid?

  1. Ineffective coping strategies: Emotional suppression, isolation, using work excessively as an escape.
  2. A self-sacrificing mindset: Avoid beliefs such as “If I don’t do it, no one will.”
  3. Neglecting signs of distress: Do not normalise persistent exhaustion or intense distress as “part of the job”.
  4. Excessive perfectionism: Avoid pursuing perfect performance at the expense of wellbeing.

Specialist support

When to refer?

Referral to specialist support should be considered when:

  • !Symptoms persist daily for several weeks
  • !There is a significant impact on personal or professional functioning
  • !There is intense distress, hopelessness or a persistent feeling of emptiness
  • !There are risk behaviours or suicidal ideation
  • !Personal resources and informal support are no longer enough

Where to turn
