Conflict in the team

Conflict is part of human relationships and can arise even in well-functioning teams. The aim is not to eliminate conflict entirely, but to promote safe, respectful and effective ways of managing it.

This module deals with difficult situations. If you need to, take a break or use the Support button, always available at the top of the page.

7 screens

What may be happening?

Differences that create tension

Differences in perspective, communication, needs or expectations can create tension, especially in high-stress contexts.

Conflict can arise from multiple factors, including

  • differences in communication or working styles
  • a perception of unfairness or lack of recognition
  • lack of clarity about roles and responsibilities
  • accumulated stress or organisational pressure
  • unresolved interpersonal difficulties
  • different interpretations of the same situation

In situations of conflict, people commonly

  • focus on the other person’s mistakes
  • have difficulty expressing needs directly
  • interpret behaviour as more threatening
  • take rigid or defensive positions

Factors such as fatigue, noise, pressure or emotional overload can reduce tolerance for frustration and increase reactivity.

What to do?

Describe, express, assert, reinforce

The intervention should focus on reducing emotional escalation, promoting clear communication and solving the problem effectively.

Focus on the facts, objectively.

  • “We agreed to hand in this task yesterday and it still hasn’t been completed.”

Communicate feelings or the impact of the situation using first-person statements.

  • “I feel overwhelmed when this happens.”

Say clearly what you need or expect.

  • “I need you to let me know in advance if there are delays.”

Explain the benefits of collaboration and resolution.

  • “If we can align on this, the team’s work becomes easier for everyone.”

What to do?

Throughout the conversation

  1. 5
    Keep focus and emotional regulation: Avoid getting drawn into provocations or side arguments. If necessary, calmly repeat the main point and bring the conversation back to the goal.
  2. 6
    Show confidence: Use a firm tone, open posture, and clear, respectful communication.
  3. 7
    Negotiate: Look for flexible, realistic solutions, promoting collaboration rather than competition.
  4. +
    Validate without necessarily agreeing: Showing understanding of the other person’s point of view can reduce defensiveness and make dialogue easier. “I understand that this situation may also be difficult for you.”
  5. +
    Focus on resolution and not on “being right”: Prioritise effectiveness, cooperation and the team’s shared goals.

Points to watch

What to avoid?

  1. Judging or labelling: Avoid phrases such as “you’re irresponsible” or “you’re too sensitive”.
  2. Hitting back: Responding to criticism with aggression tends to increase conflict.
  3. Making assumptions: Avoid assuming intentions or motives without clarification.
  4. Giving unsolicited advice: Not every situation needs immediate solutions.
  5. Humiliating or exposing colleagues: Avoid public confrontations or belittling comments.
  6. Getting into power struggles: The focus should be on solving the problem, not on “winning” the interaction.

Specialist support

When to refer?

Referral to leadership, HR, mediation or psychological support should be considered when:

  • !There is a threat to physical or psychological safety
  • !There is hostile behaviour, harassment or intimidation
  • !The conflict is persistent and does not improve with dialogue
  • !There is a significant impact on team functioning or climate
  • !Emotional distress is starting to affect mental health or performance
  • !Attempts at internal resolution are no longer effective
