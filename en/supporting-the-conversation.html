Supporting during the conversation

Block B – A colleague is in distress. The aim is not to find the perfect words, but to create a safe context where the other person can talk, if they wish.

10 screens

The approach

How to start, how to be and how to respect their pace

Approaching a colleague in distress does not require a complex intervention, but a clear, respectful approach that fits the moment.

How to start

Be direct, simple and respectful. Mention what you have observed and leave room for a reply.

  • “I noticed you don’t seem as well and I wanted to know how you are.”
  • “I’ve noticed you seem more overwhelmed. Would you like to talk for a bit?”
A lighter alternative

Use a less direct approach if you are not that close.

  • “I just wanted to see how you are – is everything OK?”
  • “If you need to talk, I’m around.”
Posture

Keep a calm tone, without rushing or dramatising. Give the person time to reply.

  • “We can talk calmly, there’s no hurry.”
  • “I’m here to listen, with no pressure.”
Behaviour

Show presence through how you are (not only through what you say).

  • Pause during the conversation
  • Avoid interrupting
  • Keep your attention on the other person
Giving space

Respect it if the person does not want to talk at that moment.

  • “That’s fine if you don’t want to talk now.”
  • “If another time suits you better, we can talk later.”
Continuity

Show availability without pressing.

  • “If you change your mind, I’m available.”
  • “You don’t have to explain more than you want to.”

During the conversation

Listening, validating and supporting

The conversation is the central moment of the intervention. It is not about solving the situation, but about creating a safe space where the person can express themselves. These dimensions are not rigid stages, but guidelines that complement one another throughout the interaction.

What it is

Listening is more than hearing words. It means genuine attention to what the person says, to what they do not say and to how they express themselves.

The aim is not to respond quickly, but to understand.

Effective listening conveys an implicit message: “what you are going through matters and deserves attention”.

When listening, it is important to

  • Give the person time to speak, without interrupting
  • Avoid anticipating or finishing their sentences
  • Focus on what the person considers relevant
  • Accept moments of silence, without rushing to fill them

Useful phrases

  • “I’m here to listen.”
  • “You can speak at your own pace.”
  • “Would you like to tell me what has been hardest?”
  • “I’m listening, tell me.”

What it is

Validating means recognising the person’s emotional experience as legitimate, without judging, correcting or relativising it. It does not mean agreeing with everything, but showing understanding.

Validation is particularly important because it reduces the feeling of isolation and helps the person feel understood.

When validating, it is important to

  • Name or reflect emotions when appropriate
  • Avoid minimising or comparing experiences
  • Accept different ways of reacting

Useful phrases

  • “It makes sense that you feel this way.”
  • “I imagine this is hard.”
  • “I understand that this has affected you.”
  • “It’s understandable that you feel this way in this situation.”

What it is

Supporting means helping the person deal with the present moment, without taking control of the situation. The focus is on facilitating, not solving.

  • Emotional: presence, availability, stability
  • Practical: small, concrete actions (e.g. helping to make a call, finding a quiet space)
  • Looking to the near future: helping to think about the “next step”, without pressing

When supporting, it is important to

  • Ask what the person needs, instead of assuming
  • Offer concrete help, but do not impose it
  • Respect the person’s pace and decisions

Useful phrases

  • “How can I support you right now?”
  • “Is there anything that would help you now?”
  • “Would you like us to stay here for a bit?”
  • “Can I help with anything practical?”

During the conversation

Managing silence and pace

Not everyone will want to talk a lot. Some people may need time to organise what they are feeling.

In these cases:

  • 1Silence should not be seen as a failure
  • 2Presence is still a form of support
  • 3Short phrases of continuity can help

Phrases to give space

  • “You don’t have to say anything, I can just stay here with you.”
  • “We can stay in silence, if you prefer.”
  • “That’s fine if you don’t want to talk now.”
  • “You don’t have to explain more than you want to.”
  • “If you want to talk later, I’m available.”

During the conversation

Signs to watch for during the conversation

During the interaction, it is important to stay alert to possible indicators of greater vulnerability:

  • !Sudden emotional intensification (intense crying, dysregulation)
  • !Difficulty keeping their speech coherent
  • !Expressions of hopelessness or extreme exhaustion
  • !Signs of risk to themselves or to others

In these cases

It may be necessary to adjust the intervention and consider additional support.

Key message

Supporting during the conversation does not mean having answers, solutions or perfect words.

It means being present, listening carefully, validating the experience and offering support respectfully. Often, the impact lies less in what is said and more in how you are.

During the conversation: what to avoid

Helps or doesn’t help?

It is common to feel the urge to help quickly, say something “useful” or ease the discomfort. However, some responses, even with good intentions, can make communication harder and increase emotional distance.

Phrase 1 of 8

In general, these responses have three main effects

  • they invalidate the person’s experience
  • they reduce the space for expression
  • they shift the focus from the person to the one helping

    More than finding the perfect words, it is essential to avoid responses that close down, pressure or oversimplify the other person’s experience.

    Key message

    In most situations, what helps most is not a perfect answer, but an available presence, without judgement and without hurry.

    Avoiding these responses does not mean staying silent or “not knowing what to say”. It means prioritising listening, validation and the person’s pace, instead of trying to correct, explain or solve.
