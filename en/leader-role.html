The role of the leader in Psychological First Aid

The leader as a safe and human first response: four key dimensions, the importance of psychosocial risks and the Team Psychosocial Risk Radar.

The role of the leader

The leader does not play a clinical or therapeutic role

However, they are often the first person to detect signs of psychological distress and to influence the organisation’s initial response.

Their action is not aimed at diagnosing or solving the situation, but at ensuring a first response that is safe, humane and focused on support. The leader acts as a bridge between the employee’s experience and the formal support resources.

Evidence shows that early interventions, based on listening, respect and practical action, help reduce the impact of stress and prevent difficulties from getting worse.

Very important

That leaders know the resources that exist and the referral that can be made in each case.

Four key dimensions of the leader’s role

Identifying signs early

  • Observe changes from the usual pattern
  • Consider the context (e.g. workload, recent changes)
  • Cross-check different signs (behaviour, performance, interaction)
  • Watch for persistence over time
  • Avoid assumptions and check perceptions before acting

Creating psychological safety

  • Promote active listening and an absence of judgement
  • Respond consistently and respectfully
  • Reinforce that asking for help is legitimate
  • Encourage participation and expression (including quieter people)
  • Follow up on what is shared (close the loop)

Offering a first response

  • Listen without interrupting and with full attention
  • Validate the person’s experience
  • Use open questions to understand
  • Focus on immediate practical needs
  • Keep the limits of the leader’s role clear

Making support easy to reach

  • Inform about available resources (HR, EAP, occupational health)
  • Normalise the use of support (reduce stigma)
  • Help with access (clarify next steps)
  • Adjust working conditions when necessary
  • Follow up after a referral

Key message

The leader’s role is not to solve the problem, but they should not ignore it, delay the approach or risk making it worse through omission or inadequate responses.

The difference often lies in being present in a way that is available, consistent and respectful of the limits of one’s own role. A timely and appropriate intervention can reduce the intensity and duration of distress, prevent escalation to more serious situations and lessen organisational impacts.

The importance of psychosocial risks for leaders

Why they are critical for leadership

Psychosocial risks are not just a matter of individual wellbeing; they are a direct variable in performance, how teams function and organisational sustainability. They result, above all, from how work is structured and experienced day to day:

  • Excessive workload
  • Ambiguity of roles
  • Constant pressure
  • Lack of control or autonomy
  • Inconsistent or unresponsive leadership
For the leader, this means one essential thing

They are not external phenomena; they are influenced by how you lead.

Direct impact on the team and operations

When they are not identified and managed, psychosocial risks tend to show up as:

  • More absenteeism and presenteeism
  • A drop in performance and productivity
  • More mistakes, rework and incidents
  • Interpersonal conflicts
  • Turnover and loss of talent
  • Lower engagement and motivation
Attention

Often, these signs are treated as “individual problems”, when in reality they reflect dysfunctional working conditions.

Why assess psychosocial risks

Assessing is a management tool. It allows the leader to:

  • Identify signs of risk early, before they escalate
  • Make more informed decisions about priorities and workload
  • Prevent burnout and prolonged distress
  • Adjust how the team functions in a sustainable way
  • Reduce operational impact (mistakes, conflicts, absenteeism)
Put simply

Assessing early lets you intervene lightly – assessing late forces a more intense reaction.
