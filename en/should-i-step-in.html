Quick guide for leaders

“Should I step in?”

The decision to step in should be based on an integrated reading of signs, and not on a single indicator.

This checklist helps the leader to

  • identify relevant changes
  • assess impact and persistence
  • recognise signs of distress
  • decide the level of intervention needed

The aim is not to diagnose, but to see whether the situation calls for

  • observation
  • active monitoring
  • or a more structured approach

The five decision dimensions

Change, impact, distress, time and risk

When it is more than Psychological First Aid

Urgent referral

Seek specialist support immediately when the person:

  • !Presents a risk to themselves (suicidal thoughts or self-harming behaviour) or to others
  • !Is so disorganised that they cannot look after themselves
  • !Shows signs of a medical crisis
  • !Remains seriously dysregulated despite support

In these cases

The leader does not manage the situation alone: they activate resources and do not leave the person on their own.

Individual signs vs. team signs

Sometimes the sign is in the team climate

Distress does not always show up in just one person. The leader should read both levels:

Individual signs

Changes in a person’s usual functioning (isolation, drop in performance, irritability, exhaustion).

Collective / climate signs: when the distress is the team’s
  • Emotional contagion (tension or discouragement that “spreads”)
  • More conflicts or misunderstandings
  • Widespread absenteeism or presenteeism
  • Breakdown in communication and collaboration
  • An across-the-board drop in involvement or initiative

Collective signs usually point to organisational factors (workload, leadership, role clarity, recognition) and not only to individual difficulties.

See it in action

Role play 3

A conflict between two employees rarely starts as a conflict – it starts as accumulated pressure, failed communication or misaligned expectations. The moment the leader steps in makes all the difference between the conflict escalating and being resolved before it becomes a bigger problem.

ContextFruit and vegetable section, peak hour. Two employees get into an open conflict over priorities and lack of communication. The section leader steps in.

What to observeHow the leader steps in without taking sides, how she validates both perspectives and how she closes with a clear agreement.

  • The leader did not ask “whose fault was it”. Why is that choice important?
  • Identify the moment when the leader used validation – what exactly did she say?
  • How would this situation have ended if the leader had waited for the employees to sort it out on their own?
  • In your own context, what kind of conflicts do you recognise in this scenario?

From doubt to action: the role of the leader

The difference lies not in “knowing exactly what is going on”, but in not letting the situation develop in silence.

One of the main risks in leadership is waiting for certainty before acting. In the psychosocial context, that certainty rarely arrives – and when it does, there is often already a significant impact. Stepping in does not require a diagnosis or confirmation. It requires a willingness to approach, even with incomplete information.

  • Recognising signs
  • Do not ignore the doubt
  • Create space for a conversation
  • Adjust or refer when necessary

Phrases to start the approach

Observation + intention + openness

The approach should be non-intrusive: be available without forcing the conversation, without insisting on details and without taking away the person’s autonomy. The aim is not to have the perfect phrase, but to open the door in a safe, concrete and respectful way.

Examples

  • “I’ve noticed some changes over the past few weeks and I’d like to understand how you are.”
  • “I have a feeling this period may be demanding. How are you experiencing it?”
  • “If there’s anything making work harder, we can look at it together.”
  • “I don’t know exactly what’s going on, but I thought it was important not to ignore it and to ask how you are.”
  • “I’ve noticed a few changes and I wanted to know how you are.”

If the person does not want to talk at that moment

The leader’s role does not end – it becomes discreet presence, respect and follow-up.

Early interventions tend to be simpler, more proportionate and more effective.
